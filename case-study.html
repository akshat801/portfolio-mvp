Help Me Write

Generative writing, rebuilt around trust. From concept to 2B users across 11 locales in eighteen months.

TL;DR

We redesigned how billions of people get help writing inside Gmail and Docs. The new assist is quiet by default, rich when asked — invoked on a single shortcut, streamed as ghost text, reversible with one keystroke. Twelve weeks after launch, suggestion completion was up 2.4×, weekly users up 38%, and assist-related support tickets down 62%.

01 · Context

By early 2024, users expected generative AI to help with everyday writing — but Gmail and Docs' existing assist still read like ambitious autocomplete. The problem wasn't capability. It was trust, predictability, and a UI that didn't know when to step aside.

We were asked to rebuild the assist surface so that generative help felt like a collaborator you could ignore, not an autocomplete you had to fight.

  • Scale. 2B monthly Workspace users. Small UI changes have outsized downstream effects.
  • Stakes. Email is high-consequence writing. Wrong tone, broken undo, or lost intent damages trust fast.
  • Constraint. Ship to 11 locales in year one, on the existing Workspace assist infrastructure.

02 · Research

Over six weeks we ran diary studies with 24 participants across four markets, interviewed 12 writing‑heavy professionals, and reviewed 2,300 support tickets from the existing assist product.

Three patterns emerged:

  • Suggestion fatigue. Power users disabled assist within 10 days — the UI was too loud for drafting flow.
  • Tone mismatch. The model defaulted to formal language, which felt wrong for casual replies (78% of use).
  • Undo confusion. Users couldn't reliably return to their original text after accepting a suggestion.

03 · Strategy

We framed the design around a single principle — quiet by default, rich when asked. Assist would appear only on deliberate invocation, and once invoked, would expose enough control (tone, length, style) that the writer always stayed in charge.

A good assistant is the one you notice the moment you need it, and forget about when you don't.

Design principle · framed week four

That principle became a veto on individual decisions. Any suggestion that required the user to dismiss, disable, or undo something got reconsidered before shipping.

04 · Design

Across four sprints we explored five invocation patterns (keyboard shortcut, selection menu, sidebar, inline toolbar, command palette) and three response modes (streaming preview, full replacement, side‑by‑side diff).

The shipped solution invokes via ⌘ K or a toolbar button. Suggestions stream as ghost text the writer can accept with Tab, modify with ⌘ E, or dismiss with Esc. Tone and length controls sit quietly below the preview, reached only when wanted.

We added a diff‑aware undo so users can always roll back to their pre‑assist text — one of the highest‑confidence calls we made all year.

A six‑week gated rollout with 2% of U.S. users surfaced two copy issues and one undo edge case, which we iterated before widening to 11 locales over the following twelve weeks.

05 · Outcome

  • 2.4× Suggestion completion rate vs baseline
  • +38% Weekly active assist users
  • −62% Assist‑related support tickets
  • 11 Locales shipped in year one

Twelve weeks post‑launch · measured across Gmail and Docs.

06 · Reflection

The thing I'd change: we spent weeks polishing the streaming animation that could have been cut for launch and added in an iteration. It shipped beautifully, but the same quality bar applied to undo from day one might have caught the two post‑launch bugs.

The best call was holding the quiet by default principle as a veto. It kept the team aligned when the space got crowded with options, and the cost of saying no was always lower than it felt in the moment.
